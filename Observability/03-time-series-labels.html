Lesson 3

Time series, labels & cardinality

"A metric" is really many separate lists of numbers. Understanding that explains the most common and expensive observability mistake.

By the end you can…

Say exactly what a time series is, count how many series a metric produces, and explain why a user_id label can take down a monitoring system.

What is a time series?

A time series is a list of samples, each a pair (timestamp, value), for one unique identity. The identity is the metric name plus its set of labels (key=value pairs, also called attributes or dimensions).

http_requests_total{method="GET",  status="200"}   → (10:00:00, 1500) (10:00:15, 1534) (10:00:30, 1590) …
http_requests_total{method="GET",  status="500"}   → (10:00:00,    4) (10:00:15,    4) (10:00:30,    9) …
http_requests_total{method="POST", status="200"}   → (10:00:00,  300) (10:00:15,  311) (10:00:30,  320) …

That is three different series sharing one metric name. Change any label value and you get a new series. Labels are what let you ask "errors per method" or "only POST traffic".

Cardinality = number of series

Cardinality is how many distinct series exist. For a metric it is (roughly) the product of the number of values of each label. Active series consume resources, especially memory and index space; disk use also depends on sample rate, retention, labels and compression. This multiplication is why one careless label can be expensive.

3) 6,000,000 × 3 KB = 18,000,000 KB ≈ 18 GB — from one metric under the exercise's assumed cost. Avoid high-cardinality metric labels; if correlation requires an identifier, use an approved opaque or pseudonymous value in protected logs or traces.

Good label vs bad label

Good: small, bounded sets — method, status_code, region, service.
Bad (unbounded): user_id, email, request_id, raw URLs with IDs in them (/orders/48213 — use the route template /orders/{id}), timestamps.
