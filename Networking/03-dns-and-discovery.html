DNS and service discovery

You changed the record. Some listeners still reach the old region. That is not necessarily a broken load balancer.

By the end you can...

Distinguish authoritative answers from cached answers and explain DNS-based failover's limits.

Who answers?

An application asks a stub resolver; a recursive resolver finds and caches answers from authoritative DNS servers. A/AAAA records supply IPv4/IPv6 addresses; CNAME points to another name. Provider alias records are provider-specific conveniences, not a universal DNS record type.

The TTL tells caches how long an answer may be retained. NXDOMAIN and other negative answers can also be cached. OS, runtime and application caches add behavior; some clients retain addresses longer than expected. Existing connections do not move when a record changes.

Use documented endpoint names rather than pinning a managed proxy's observed IP. Choose an explicitly supported static-IP service when an allowlist requires it. DNS rotation is not per-request balancing: one client can reuse one connection for thousands of requests.

Split-horizon DNS gives different answers inside and outside a network. Hybrid clients need forwarding rules and reachability to the intended resolver. A private endpoint without working private DNS often leaves clients trying the public path.

Discovery is not reachability

DNS or a service registry tells you where to try. Routes, policy, listener health and application authorization determine whether that attempt works. DNS health checks also need an appropriate vantage point; public probes cannot automatically test arbitrary private addresses.
