Lesson 6

KEDA and event-driven scaling

A queue consumer with an empty queue uses no CPU, so an HPA sees a perfectly healthy idle service — while a million messages pile up. KEDA fixes the signal, and incidentally gets you to zero replicas.

By the end you can…

Explain what a ScaledObject does, describe the split between the activation phase and the scaling phase, say why KEDA creates an HPA rather than replacing it, and reason about the default polling and cooldown values.

The problem, concretely

A worker reads from a queue. 50,000 messages arrive at once. Each Pod handles maybe 10 a second, so you want many Pods — now.

Point an HPA at CPU and nothing happens. The existing Pods are not CPU-bound; they are waiting on IO, which is lesson 5's failure mode exactly. The number that matters — how much work is waiting — lives in the queue, outside the cluster, and no Kubernetes metric knows about it.

KEDA's job is to get that number into Kubernetes. The official Kubernetes documentation describes it as a CNCF-graduated project enabling you to scale your workloads based on the number of events to be processed, for example the amount of messages in a queue, with a wide range of adapters for different event sources to choose from.

The ScaledObject

You do not write an HPA. You write a ScaledObject pointing at your workload and naming a trigger:

apiVersion: keda.sh/v1alpha1
kind: ScaledObject
metadata:
  name: order-worker
spec:
  scaleTargetRef:
    name: order-worker        # an existing Deployment
  minReplicaCount: 0          # default 0 — idle all the way down
  maxReplicaCount: 30         # default 100
  pollingInterval: 30         # seconds, default 30
  cooldownPeriod: 300         # seconds, default 300
  triggers:
    - type: rabbitmq
      metadata:
        queueName: orders
        queueLength: "20"     # messages per Pod you are willing to tolerate

queueLength: "20" is the one to understand. It is not a threshold — it is a target per replica, which makes it the desiredMetricValue in lesson 5's formula. 600 messages waiting with a target of 20 gives ceil[600 / 20] = 30 replicas.

The defaults are worth memorising because they explain most surprises:

FieldDefaultMeaning
pollingInterval30sHow often KEDA checks the trigger source
cooldownPeriod300sWait after the last trigger activity before scaling to zero
minReplicaCount0Floor KEDA scales down to
maxReplicaCount100Ceiling for the target workload
initialCooldownPeriod0sDelay before the cooldown clock starts after creation

Two phases, two controllers

This is the part that makes KEDA click, and the documentation is explicit about the split.

PhaseRangeWho decides
Activation0 ↔ 1KEDA. The activating (or deactivating) phase is the moment when KEDA (operator) has to decide if the workload should be scaled from/to zero.
Scaling1 ↔ NThe HPA. The scaling phase is the moment when KEDA has decided to scale out to 1 instance and now it is the HPA controller who takes the scaling decisions.

So KEDA acts to monitor the event source and feed that data to Kubernetes and the HPA (Horizontal Pod Autoscaler) to drive rapid scale of a resource. It creates an HPA for you and serves it the queue depth through the external metrics API — the third row of lesson 5's table.

Two things follow from that, and both save real debugging time:

  • Everything from lesson 5 still applies between 1 and N — the formula, the 10% tolerance, the 300-second scale-down stabilization. KEDA did not replace any of it.
  • kubectl get hpa shows an HPA named after your ScaledObject that you did not create. Do not edit it. KEDA owns it and will reconcile your changes away.

And it explains the one piece KEDA genuinely adds: an HPA cannot reliably take you to zero (lesson 5's feature gate), so KEDA handles 0↔1 itself and delegates the rest.

Watch both phases

Queue depth to replicas

Picking the trigger is the same skill as picking an SLI

Queue depth works as a scaling signal for the reason a good SLI works: it measures the thing users actually suffer from. Observability lesson 9 is the same reasoning applied to alerting.

And the same trap appears. Queue depth tells you work is waiting, not how fast you are draining it. A queue at a steady 500 with consumers keeping pace is fine; a queue at 500 and climbing is an incident. For Kafka, consumer lag is usually the better trigger for exactly this reason — it is a rate-like signal rather than a level.

What scale-to-zero actually costs

Zero replicas is the headline feature and it is not free. The first message after an idle period waits for the whole chain: poll interval, then Pod scheduling, then image pull, then your application's startup.

message arrives
  → up to 30s        waiting for the next poll (pollingInterval)
  → seconds          scheduling (lesson 4) — longer if a node is needed (lesson 7)
  → seconds–minutes  image pull on a cold node
  → your startup time

Tens of seconds is normal, and minutes is possible if a node has to be provisioned first. That is fine for a nightly batch job and unacceptable for anything a user is waiting on. Reach for minReplicaCount: 1 when latency matters more than the idle cost.

Two other things to know before running it in production:

  • cooldownPeriod only applies to the last step. It is the wait after trigger activity stops before going to zero — not a general scale-down delay. Between 1 and N you are on the HPA's stabilization window.
  • Set fallback. If a scaler cannot reach its source — the broker is down, credentials expired — you want a defined replica count rather than undefined behaviour at the worst moment.

✍️ Do it by hand

A ScaledObject with queueLength: "25", minReplicaCount: 0, maxReplicaCount: 20, and defaults elsewhere.

  1. The queue holds 300 messages. How many replicas, and which controller decided?
  2. The queue goes from 0 to 10. What happens, and who did it?
  3. The queue holds 900. How many replicas, and what should you be alerted about?
  4. The queue empties at 12:00. When does the Deployment reach zero?
  5. A teammate edits the HPA KEDA created, raising maxReplicas. What happens?
Show answer

1) ceil[300 / 25] = 12. The workload was already above zero, so this is the scaling phase — the HPA decided, using lesson 5's formula.

2) KEDA activates the workload from 0 to 1. This is the one transition the HPA cannot be relied on for, and the reason KEDA exists as more than a metrics adapter. The jump from 1 to the formula's answer then follows from the HPA.

3) ceil[900 / 25] = 36, clamped to 20 by maxReplicaCount. Being pinned at the ceiling means the queue is growing faster than you can drain it — alert on it, because the autoscaler has nothing left to give.

4) Around 12:05: the default cooldownPeriod is 300 seconds after the last trigger activity, and the next poll is up to 30 seconds after that.

5) It is reverted. KEDA owns that HPA and reconciles it back — lesson 1's loop, now working against you. Change maxReplicaCount on the ScaledObject instead.
