Pod networking and the CNI

Kubernetes writes down demanding rules about how Pods must reach each other, then ships nothing that implements them. That hole is deliberate, and the thing you drop into it — your CNI plugin — decides your IP plan, your blast radius, your security model and, eventually, one of your worst outages.

How each lesson works

  1. Idea in plain words with a tiny example.
  2. Play with an animation or interactive widget.
  3. Do it by hand — a short exercise with a hidden answer.
  4. Quiz — pass it to complete the lesson.

Lessons

    Why this is a side course, not lesson 10

    The main course follows one thread: the control loop, applied to Pods, nodes, replicas and custom resources. Networking is a different thread that runs underneath all of it — every lesson there quietly assumed Pods could already talk to each other.

    This is where that assumption gets paid for. You can read it after lesson 4 of the main course; nothing here needs the autoscaling lessons.

    What you will be able to do

    Say what breaks if you install no CNI at all, and read the ContainerCreating error that results. Size a Pod CIDR before it strands you three months into a cluster's life. Trace a packet from one Pod's network namespace to another's and say where encapsulation was added and what it cost in MTU. Write a NetworkPolicy that does what you meant, and tell whether your plugin will enforce it at all. Choose between Flannel, Calico, Cilium and a cloud CNI with reasons you can defend in a design review.

    Before you start

    Do lessons 1 and 2 of the Kubernetes course first — this one assumes you know what a Pod, a DaemonSet and a controller are. Lesson 4 here refers back to scheduling when IP exhaustion starts producing Pending Pods that look like a scheduler problem and are not.
