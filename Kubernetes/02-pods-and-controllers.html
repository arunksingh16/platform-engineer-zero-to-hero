Lesson 2

Pods, and the controllers that run them

A Pod is the thing Kubernetes actually runs, and it is more fragile than most people expect — it cannot move, cannot heal, and cannot be upgraded. Everything that makes workloads durable is a controller stacked on top.

By the end you can…

Say what a Pod is and what it is not, explain the Deployment → ReplicaSet → Pod chain, and work out how many Pods exist at each step of a rolling update from maxSurge and maxUnavailable.

What a Pod actually is

A Pod is one or more containers that are always scheduled together on one node, sharing a network namespace and optionally some volumes. Containers in a Pod reach each other on localhost and share one IP address.

Most Pods hold exactly one container. Multiple containers are for helpers that genuinely cannot be separated — a sidecar that tails the app's log file, a proxy sharing its network. If two things could run on different machines, they want to be two Pods.

Now the parts people get wrong:

A Pod is not…Because
movableOnce scheduled to a node it stays there for life. "Rescheduling" always means deleting it and creating a different one.
repairableMost of its spec is immutable. Changing the image means a new Pod, not an updated one.
durableIf its node dies, a bare Pod is simply gone. Nothing restores it.
stable in identityIts name and IP belong to that instance only. The replacement gets new ones.

So a bare Pod is a one-shot. That is why you almost never write one — you write something that manages Pods, and lesson 1's loop does the rest.

The chain

Three objects, three loops, each watching the one below:

Deployment      "I want version v2, rolled out safely"
   └── ReplicaSet   "I want 3 Pods matching this exact template"
        └── Pod         "I am one running instance"
ObjectIts one job
ReplicaSetKeep exactly N Pods alive matching one fixed template. It cannot change the template.
DeploymentOwn several ReplicaSets and shift replicas between them, which is how versions change.

That division is the key to rollouts. A ReplicaSet is deliberately incapable of updating anything — so to change the image, the Deployment makes a second ReplicaSet with the new template and moves replicas across. The docs put it plainly: A new ReplicaSet is created, and the Deployment gradually scales it up while scaling down the old ReplicaSet, ensuring Pods are replaced at a controlled rate.

The old ReplicaSet is kept, scaled to zero. That is what a rollback is: scale the old one back up. revisionHistoryLimit controls how many are retained, and defaults to 10.

The two numbers that control a rollout

"At a controlled rate" means two knobs, both defaulting to 25%:

FieldMeaningDefault
maxSurgeThe maximum number of pods that can be scheduled above the desired number of pods. Percentages round up.25%
maxUnavailableThe maximum number of pods that can be unavailable during the update. Percentages round down.25%

Together they bound the Pod count for the whole rollout. With 4 replicas and the defaults: at most 5 Pods exist at once (4 + 25%), and at least 3 are available (4 − 25%). The rollout proceeds in whatever steps keep both true.

The two edge cases are worth knowing because they are the ones people reach for:

  • maxUnavailable: 0 — never drop below full capacity. Requires maxSurge ≥ 1, and needs spare room in the cluster for the extra Pods.
  • maxSurge: 0 — never exceed the replica count, useful when capacity or licences are fixed. Requires maxUnavailable ≥ 1, so you take a capacity dip.
  • Both 0 is rejected — it describes a rollout that cannot take a single step.

If a rollout stops making progress for progressDeadlineSeconds (default 600), the Deployment is marked failed. It does not roll back on its own.

Deployments are not the only option

They assume Pods are interchangeable. When they are not, the other controllers exist for good reasons:

StatefulSet — stable names (db-0, db-1) and stable storage, updated in order. For databases and anything doing leader election.
DaemonSet — one Pod per node, automatically on new nodes. For log shippers, CNI and node agents. This one matters in lesson 7: every node Karpenter adds costs you one Pod of every DaemonSet.
Job / CronJob — run to completion, once or on a schedule.

✍️ Do it by hand

A Deployment has replicas: 10, maxSurge: 25%, maxUnavailable: 25%. You change the image.

  1. What are maxSurge and maxUnavailable as absolute numbers? Mind the rounding.
  2. What is the most Pods that can exist at once? The fewest available?
  3. At the very first step, how many new Pods can be created before anything is torn down?
  4. You set maxUnavailable: 0 and the cluster is full. What happens?
  5. The team wants zero capacity loss and no extra Pods. What do you tell them?
Show answer

1) maxSurge rounds up: 25% of 10 = 2.5 → 3. maxUnavailable rounds down: 2.5 → 2. The asymmetry is deliberate — both defaults err towards keeping capacity.

2) At most 13 Pods (10 + 3). At least 8 available (10 − 2).

3) 3 — the surge allowance. It can also delete up to 2 old Pods straight away, since that still leaves 8 available.

4) The rollout stalls. New Pods cannot be created because there is nowhere to put them, and no old Pod may be removed first. It sits there until capacity appears or progressDeadlineSeconds (default 600) marks it failed. This is a very common real-world stall, and lesson 7 is how clusters avoid it.

5) It is impossible — both at 0 is rejected, because the rollout could never take a step. They must choose: a brief capacity dip, or temporary extra Pods.
