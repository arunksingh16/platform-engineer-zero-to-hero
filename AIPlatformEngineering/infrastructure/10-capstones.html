Lesson 10 / Defend the design

Design review and incident capstones

Bring the workload, arithmetic, topology and recovery plan together. There can be several good architectures; there should not be unexplained assumptions.

Your deliverable

Produce two short design documents with a diagram, explicit sizing assumptions, benchmark plan, failure response and cost estimate. Then rehearse a five-minute design defense for each.

1. Training platform: meet the deadline

A research team needs a full training run of a dense 7-billion-parameter model over 10 billion tokens. The deadline is 96 hours from job start. Quality and global batch must remain comparable across candidate configurations. Several other teams share the platform.

Given for this fictional exerciseValue
Candidate accelerator memory80 GiB per device; supported framework and precision assumed
Illustrative persistent state16 bytes/parameter, including weights
Baseline useful training throughput20,000 tokens/s on 8 GPUs
Candidate16 GPUs, 85% relative scaling efficiency
Combined evaluation/checkpoint/recovery allowance8 allocated hours
Accelerator allocation price$3/GPU-hour
Checkpoint size / effective durable write800 GB / 4 GB/s

The checkpoint size is an independent supplied operational measurement, potentially including additional state; do not infer it from the simplified parameter budget. Treat throughput and overhead as invented inputs requiring validation before a real commitment.

Design tasks

  1. Explain why unsharded persistent state does not fit one candidate device. Propose a sharding/parallel layout and a peak-memory validation plan.
  2. Draw nodes, accelerator groups, collective fabric, dataset source and durable checkpoint path. Label failure boundaries and staging caches.
  3. Calculate runtime and allocation cost. Compare with the 8-GPU baseline, assuming the same 8-hour allowance.
  4. Define admission, priority and recovery behavior so a partial worker group does not strand resources.
  5. State what you would measure before promising the deadline.
Reference design and calculations

Persistent state = 7×10⁹×16 / 2³⁰ = 104.31 GiB, already larger than 80 GiB. A feasible candidate is fully sharded data parallelism across 16 ranks on two tightly connected eight-GPU nodes, assuming the platform supports this topology. Ideal persistent state is about 6.52 GiB/rank; that figure alone does not establish peak fit. Measure gathered groups, activations, buffers, loading and checkpoint peaks with the actual sequence length.

Place the job's ranks together on an appropriate collective fabric, stage versioned dataset shards locally, and store committed checkpoints outside both nodes. Admit the required group together. Keep global batch constant by adjusting per-rank microbatch or accumulation where valid, and check training quality.

Candidate throughput = 20,000 × 16/8 × 0.85 = 34,000 tokens/s. Runtime = 10×10⁹ / 34,000 / 3,600 + 8 = 89.70 hours. Cost = runtime × 16 × $3 = $4,305.57. The baseline takes 138.89 + 8 = 146.89 hours, costing $3,525.33. The candidate costs more overall but meets the deadline under these assumptions.

Checkpoint write lower bound is 800/4 = 200 seconds. Ensure total measured overhead fits the supplied 8-hour allowance. Test restoring on replacement workers, including provisioning and read time. Evaluate input and collective throughput at 16 GPUs; verify available capacity and admission timing separately from the job-start deadline.

Use the cluster diagram as a topology template; its two-GPU boxes are symbolic, so label the eight actual devices per node in your submitted design.

Training incident: the deadline is now at risk

Six hours into the run, step time grows. Collective time rises sharply on one node while input wait and checkpoint duration remain stable. A NIC error counter is increasing.

Response and trade-off

Identify affected ranks and the first error, correlate NIC and collective telemetry, and compare with the healthy-node baseline. Preserve a valid checkpoint if possible. Quarantine the suspect path/node using the platform workflow, restore the job group on known-good capacity and confirm throughput. Recalculate remaining work and recovery time against the deadline. Continuing with a persistently slow fabric may cost more than a controlled restart; restarting without a usable checkpoint may be worse.

2. Inference platform: survive a failure at peak

The coding assistant already uses a selected serving engine. It must serve 12 requests/s at peak for the benchmarked prompt/output mix. Targets: p95 TTFT < 1.5 s, p95 per-request mean TPOT < 50 ms and errors < 0.5%.

Given for this fictional exerciseValue
Complete replica2 collaborating GPUs; memory and quality tests pass
Measured service-compliant capacity4 requests/s per ready replica
Planning fraction75% of that capacity
Failure objectiveLoss of any one replica-hosting node
Cold startup5 minutes including provisioning and warm-up
Expected burst notice2 minutes
Accelerator allocation price$3/GPU-hour

Design tasks

  1. Calculate serving replicas, spare replicas, total GPUs and hourly allocation cost.
  2. Draw complete replicas and node boundaries. Explain the node-size assumption and how spare paid devices affect real cost.
  3. Define routing, readiness, admission limits and scaling signals.
  4. Explain why reactive cold scaling cannot cover the entire burst.
  5. Describe a rollout and a node-failure test that preserve the service targets.
Reference design and calculations

Planned per-replica rate = 4 × 0.75 = 3 requests/s. Serving replicas = ceil(12/3) = 4. Add one spare = 5 complete replicas, or 10 GPUs, costing $30/hour for the modeled accelerator allocation.

Put one complete two-GPU replica on each of five distinct nodes, assuming supported two-GPU nodes are available. Keep each replica's workers local. If only larger nodes are purchasable, account for all paid capacity and re-evaluate packing: placing two replicas on a node makes a single-node loss remove two replicas and invalidates the one-spare arithmetic. This design promises node-failure tolerance, not zone-failure tolerance.

Use readiness after model load and warm-up, route only to ready replicas, and balance cache locality against queue concentration. Bound waiting and retries. Maintain the already-warm reserve and pre-scale known demand: a five-minute cold start cannot fully respond to two-minute notice. Monitor queued work, TTFT/TPOT, errors, lengths and resource metrics.

After one node fails, four replicas remain. Their 16 requests/s measured boundary leaves 12 requests/s at the chosen 75% planning fraction. Test the failure with representative traffic, cold caches and retries; aggregate arithmetic alone does not prove p95 latency. During upgrades, avoid consuming the failure reserve unless the operating policy explicitly accepts that temporary reduction, or add surge capacity.

Expand the two-replica diagram to five independent node boundaries in your design.

Inference incident: memory fits but first tokens are late

A new client sends much longer prompts. GPU memory remains below its limit, but p95 TTFT rises. A teammate suggests increasing the queue limit.

Response and trade-off

Compare input lengths, arrival rates, prefill time, cache reuse and queue wait with the benchmark contract. Longer prompts change the workload and can reduce replica capacity even when memory fits. Bound admission, isolate the workload or revise validated capacity and add appropriate ready resources. A larger queue may merely hide overload while increasing wait. Re-benchmark the new mix and update the capacity plan.

3. Design-review rubric

Score each design from 0–2 per row: 0 missing/incorrect, 1 present but unvalidated, 2 explicit and supported by the supplied calculation or a concrete measurement plan. A score of 8/10 with no zero is a useful practice target, not a certification.

CriterionEvidence for 2 points
Workload contractModel/work mix, quality, deadline or service targets and failure objective are explicit
SizingCorrect units and arithmetic, memory assumptions and measurable capacity boundary
ArchitectureCompute, data and communication paths plus labeled failure boundaries
OperationsAdmission, observability, recovery and rollout with specific validation steps
Economics and uncertaintyAllocation versus total cost, supply constraints and decision-changing evidence
