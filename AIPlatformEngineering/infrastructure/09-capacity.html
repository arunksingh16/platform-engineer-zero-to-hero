Lesson 9 / Demand → measured capacity → supply

Capacity planning and economics

A capacity plan connects a workload to an achievable service outcome, an obtainable fleet and a budget. “We need 100 GPUs” skips the important reasoning.

By the end you can…

Calculate training time and inference replica counts, include failure and operating margin explicitly, and distinguish a scenario estimate from a validated plan.

1. Training: work divided by useful rate

candidate tokens/s = baseline tokens/s × candidate GPUs / baseline GPUs
                     × relative scaling efficiency
wall hours = token budget / candidate tokens/s / 3600 + overhead hours
accelerator allocation cost = wall hours × candidate GPUs × $/GPU-hour

Use measured throughput for the model, sequence length, global batch and parallel configuration. Relative scaling efficiency describes a candidate versus the linear projection from that baseline, not model FLOP utilization. It must be measured or explicitly treated as an assumption. The planner includes checkpoint, evaluation and expected recovery time through the overhead field; do not count them again if already included in the baseline rate.

Training deadline planner

Fictional baseline: 8 GPUs sustain 20,000 training tokens/s during useful training. A 16-GPU candidate has 85% relative scaling efficiency. This example supports arithmetic, not a hardware recommendation.

34,000 tokens/s → 89.70 hours including overhead; $4,305.57 accelerator allocation cost. Meets a 96-hour deadline.

Changing GPU count does not automatically adjust efficiency or memory feasibility. Re-benchmark each candidate layout. The bounded planner excludes superlinear speedups; use direct measured results for such cases.

Default arithmetic: 10×10⁹ / 34,000 / 3,600 = 81.70 compute hours; add 8 = 89.70. Sixteen GPUs at $3/hour cost $48/hour. Admission wait and procurement lead time are outside this job-start deadline: add them when planning a calendar delivery date. For multiple jobs, include arrival schedules, overlap, queue policy, fragmentation and recovery capacity.

2. Inference: serve demand after the planned failure

serving replicas = ceil(peak requests/s / (measured replica capacity × target fraction))
provisioned replicas = serving replicas + replicas lost in planned failure
GPUs = provisioned replicas × GPUs per complete replica

Here target fraction is a planning fraction of already service-target-compliant capacity, not GPU utilization. For example, 75% leaves load margin beneath the measured boundary. Failure spare replicas are a separate addition. Avoid applying an unexplained “headroom percentage” multiple times.

Inference fleet planner

4 serving + 1 failure-spare = 5 replicas; 10 GPUs; $30/hour accelerator allocation.

Assumes identical ready replicas and a placement where the declared failure removes at most the entered count. Does not prove zone resilience or latency under a changed workload. Model whole-zone loss using the actual number of replicas lost there.

Little's Law, L = λW, relates average in-system work to arrival rate and average time in a stable system. At 12 requests/s and a mean 5-second residence time, average in-system concurrency is 60. It does not predict p95 latency or establish GPU capacity; overload is not a stable queue.

3. Check that the capacity exists

  • Quota: permission to consume resources up to a limit; check family, region and account/project boundaries.
  • Physical supply: obtainable hardware in the required topology and time window.
  • Reservation: a product-specific capacity arrangement with scope and matching requirements.
  • Discount commitment: a pricing arrangement; do not assume it reserves hardware.
  • Ready capacity: admitted, provisioned, loaded and warmed capacity available to do useful work.

Build a demand forecast with baseline, peak and growth scenarios, plus upgrade/recovery requirements. Track requested, admitted, allocated and useful capacity separately. For self-managed infrastructure include rack power, cooling, switch ports, delivery lead times and replacement stock.

4. Count cost across the system

Include hosts, accelerators, persistent and scratch storage, data requests, network transfer, fabric, idle reservations, failed work and platform operations. The calculator's dollars represent accelerator allocation only, with an invented rate. A bundled VM price should be allocated consistently rather than counting host charges twice.

Useful training measures include dollars per completed valid run and time to result. Useful inference measures include dollars per successful request meeting targets, with a fixed workload distribution. Report idle headroom separately so reliability choices are visible rather than mislabeled as waste.

Work it yourself

Default inference demand doubles to 24 requests/s. Keep capacity 4, planning fraction 75%, one replica spare, two GPUs/replica and $3/GPU-hour.

Worked answer

ceil(24 / 3) = 8 serving replicas. Add one = 9 replicas, or 18 GPUs, at $54/hour. Validate quota, topology, shared artifact/storage capacity and the actual failure placement before calling this deployable.

Interview practice

“How many GPUs do we need next quarter?”

A strong answer

I would forecast workload-specific demand, benchmark feasible configurations, model overlapping training deadlines and inference service targets, add explicit failure/upgrade capacity, and verify supply. I would give a range with assumptions and a measurement plan, not one unexplained inventory number.
