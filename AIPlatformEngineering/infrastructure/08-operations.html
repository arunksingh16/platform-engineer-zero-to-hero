Lesson 8 / Turn capacity into a reliable platform

Scheduling and operating AI workloads

Four GPUs are free, yet a four-GPU Pod is Pending. The dashboard counts inventory; the scheduler needs a feasible placement.

By the end you can…

Explain device allocation and fragmentation, distinguish coordinated job admission from ordinary Pod scheduling, and diagnose an AI workload from evidence.

1. Device allocation is only the first layer

In the conventional device-plugin model, a vendor plugin advertises healthy devices to the kubelet as extended resources. Pods request integer device resources. GPU limits can supply the request by default; if both are set, they must match. The exact resource name depends on the plugin. A normal Pod is scheduled to one node, so its GPUs cannot be assembled from free devices on several nodes.

Also satisfy CPU, host RAM, labels, taints, storage and topology constraints. Kubelet Topology Manager coordinates supported local resource hints, such as NUMA alignment. It does not by itself schedule a whole distributed job near other nodes. Dynamic Resource Allocation provides another evolving device-allocation path; check Kubernetes, driver and feature support rather than assuming every cluster uses it.

2. Admit jobs, not just individual workers

A distributed training job may need all ranks to start together. Starting some workers while others wait can strand allocated devices. Queueing and coordinated admission systems can reserve a feasible worker group, enforce team quotas and integrate topology constraints. Gang scheduling coordinates placement/start conditions; a job controller handles worker lifecycle and restart policy. These are related responsibilities, not synonyms.

Kueue and Volcano are examples to investigate, with different integration and scheduling models. Kubernetes alone does not imply that any particular training controller, queue manager or topology policy is installed. Define priority, borrowing, preemption and fairness deliberately. Preempting a long job is economical only when checkpoint and restart costs are understood.

3. Sharing and isolation have different meanings

Whole-device allocation is simple but may waste capacity on small jobs. Hardware partitioning can expose isolated accelerator slices on supported products. Time-slicing multiplexes execution but does not automatically provide the same memory or fault isolation. Advertised logical resource counts are not necessarily physical GPU counts. Document which sharing mode each pool uses and benchmark interference.

Separate latency-sensitive inference from disruptive experiments when interference threatens targets. Use quotas and admission policies for fairness, and track ownership by team and workload. A utilization improvement that destroys latency or job predictability is not a platform improvement.

4. Observe the workload and its dependencies

LayerEvidence to collectQuestion it answers
TrainingStep duration, tokens/s, data wait, collective time, checkpoint duration, progressWhere is useful work waiting?
InferenceTTFT, TPOT, completion latency, queues, rejection/error rate, cache useAre users getting acceptable service?

PlatformPending reasons, admission wait, fragmentation, ready capacity, startup/recovery timeCan inventory become useful capacity in time?

Correlate by workload, node, rank or replica and deployment version. Preserve comparable baselines. High GPU activity can include inefficient kernels or waiting patterns; low activity can reflect input starvation or simply low demand. Neither alone establishes waste.

5. Recover and upgrade deliberately

  1. Training worker failure: identify the first failing rank, stop or replace the job group according to controller policy, restore the last complete checkpoint and confirm progress. Elastic recovery depends on framework and workload support.
  2. Inference worker failure: remove the affected complete replica from service, route within surviving capacity, and bound retries while replacing it.
  3. Suspect device: quarantine through the supported device/node workflow, collect diagnostics and avoid repeatedly scheduling jobs onto it.
  4. Upgrade: validate the driver, firmware, runtime, framework and model combination on a canary pool; run representative training and inference checks, then roll with rollback and spare capacity.

A PodDisruptionBudget helps constrain supported voluntary evictions; it does not prevent hardware failure or create replacement GPUs. Training checkpoint policy and inference draining still need explicit implementation.

Incident drill

After adding nodes, training tokens/s drops and collective time rises. Storage latency is unchanged. What is your next test?

Worked answer

Compare rank placement and per-link/collective behavior with the old topology. Re-run a controlled job within the original fast domain and across the new nodes. Check NIC selection, link errors and congestion. Keep model and batch settings fixed so the comparison isolates the placement change.

Interview practice

“How do you run a shared GPU platform?”

A strong answer

I separate allocation, job admission, topology, isolation and workload SLOs. I define pool and queue policies, measure fragmentation and wait time, test recovery and upgrades, and attribute cost to successful work rather than raw GPU occupancy.
