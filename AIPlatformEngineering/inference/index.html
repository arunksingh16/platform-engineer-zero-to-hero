4 lessons / Measure, then tune

Inference engineering

An inference service is the rare platform where the bottleneck is physics you can calculate in advance. Before you touch a tuning flag, you can work out on paper which phase of a request waits on memory bandwidth, which waits on arithmetic, and therefore which optimisations can possibly help you.

Your outcome

Derive your own arithmetic intensity figure, explain an engine's scheduler from its design rather than its marketing, measure a change in a way a critic can reproduce, and defend an engine choice with evidence.

Why this order

The sequence is deliberate, and it is not the order these topics usually get listed in.

Roofline first

Every later optimisation is a move on one plot. Quantisation attacks bandwidth-bound decode. Batching raises arithmetic intensity toward the compute roof. Speculative decoding spends cheap FLOPs to avoid sequential memory loads. Without this model the rest is a bag of tricks.

Measurement before knobs

Instrumentation arrives before quantisation, speculative decoding or disaggregation. Nothing after it is verifiable without it, and a credible public benchmark is mostly a credible measurement harness with a model attached.

Read, do not reimplement

Paged attention and continuous batching already exist in vLLM and SGLang. Reading the block manager until you can explain from its design why memory waste drops below 4% teaches more than rebuilding it badly.

Before you begin

You should be comfortable with Python, with transformers at the architecture level, and with a command line. No CUDA, kernel or serving background is assumed. Bring a calculator: several lessons ask you to produce your own number rather than read one.

Three handbook tracks sit underneath this one and are not re-taught here:

  • AI infrastructure at scale — accelerator selection, memory budgets and inference fleet sizing: replicas, routing, cold starts and capacity. This course works inside one replica; that one designs the fleet around it.
  • Observability — histograms, quantiles, labels and SLOs. Lesson 3 uses them rather than re-explaining them.
  • Kubernetes autoscaling and KEDA — the mechanism that will eventually scale this service on queue depth instead of CPU.

    How to use this in an interview

    State the workload and the service targets first: model, input and output token lengths, arrival rate, and the latency you are allowed to spend. Then derive, out loud, which phase your bottleneck is in. Name the measurement that would confirm it and the one that would refute it. Quote numbers as either measured on named hardware or hand-derived, and never blur the two. Describe production experience separately and honestly from lab work.
