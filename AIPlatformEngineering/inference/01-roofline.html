Lesson 1 / The mental model

Where the time actually goes

Your 8B endpoint streams about 37 tokens per second to a single user on a 24 GB GPU. A colleague proposes a card with three times the arithmetic throughput and the same memory bandwidth. Will it help? You can answer that on paper, before anyone spends a rupee, and the calculation takes about five minutes.

By the end you can…

Derive the arithmetic intensity of a decode step and a prefill pass for your own model, compare it against your GPU's ridge point, and say which phase is bandwidth-bound and why — then predict which optimisations can possibly help.

1. One request, two completely different workloads

A single completion is not one piece of work. It is one pass over the input followed by hundreds of tiny sequential passes, and those two halves stress opposite parts of the machine.

  • Prefill reads the whole prompt at once. Every input token is available simultaneously, so the GPU does one large matrix multiply per layer over all of them. Lots of arithmetic.
  • Decode produces one token, appends it to the input, and does it again. Token n+1 cannot start before token n exists, so each step is a tall thin matrix multiply over a single position. Almost no arithmetic, and the entire model has to be read from memory to do it.

The KV cache is what stops decode re-reading the whole prompt every step: the key and value vectors each token produced are kept so attention can look back at them without recomputing. Treat it as working notes the engine keeps per in-flight request. They are not free — they occupy GPU memory and they have to be read back every step.

2. The only calculation you need

Arithmetic intensity is the ratio of work done to data moved, in FLOP per byte:

intensity = floating point operations / bytes moved between HBM and the compute units

Compare it against your hardware's ridge point: peak FLOP per second divided by peak bytes per second. Below the ridge, you are waiting on memory and extra arithmetic capacity is wasted. Above it, you are waiting on arithmetic and extra bandwidth is wasted. That is the whole roofline model.

Take an 8B model in bf16 — 8.03 billion parameters at 2 bytes each, so 16 GB of weights. Use the Llama-3.1-8B shape: 32 layers, 8 key/value heads after grouped-query attention, head dimension 128. Its KV cache costs

2 (K and V) × 8 heads × 128 dim × 2 bytes × 32 layers = 131,072 bytes = 128 KB per token

So a 2,000-token context is 256 MB of KV cache for one request. Hold on to that number; it decides almost everything later.

Decode, one request at a time

A forward pass costs roughly two FLOPs per parameter per token (one multiply, one add). For a single token:

The decode floor is simply 16.26 GB divided by the bandwidth: the time to stream the weights once, which you cannot go faster than. Single-stream decode runs at roughly 1 FLOP per byte against a ridge of 150 to 420 — two to three orders of magnitude under the roof. The compute units are idle, waiting for weights to arrive.

Now answer the opening question

Three times the arithmetic throughput, same 600 GB/s of bandwidth. Decode's floor is 16.26 GB ÷ 600 GB/s = 27 ms per token whatever the FLOPs are, so single-user streaming stays at about 37 tokens per second. Prefill would get faster. For a chat workload that spends 87% of its wall clock in decode, you would be paying for a roof you never reach.

Prefill, the same model

Now process 2,000 input tokens in one pass. The weights are still read once, but every one of them is used 2,000 times over:

QuantityValue
Arithmetic2 × 8.03B × 2,000 tokens = 32.1 TFLOP
Bytes moved16 GB of weights, plus 0.26 GB of KV written
Intensity≈ 1,970 FLOP/byte

Nearly two thousand FLOP per byte, against ridges of 153 to 419. Prefill sits comfortably above every roof in that table: it is compute-bound. Its floor on an A100 is 32.1 TFLOP ÷ 312 TFLOP/s = 103 ms, and the 380 ms in the diagram above reflects achieving roughly a quarter of peak, which is an ordinary result for a single 2,000-token prefill.

Two phases, three orders of magnitude apart in intensity, sharing one GPU. Every architectural idea in the rest of this course follows from that sentence.

3. The lever that moves decode: batch size

Decode is bandwidth-bound because 16 GB of weights were read to produce a single token. Read them once and produce 64 tokens — one for each of 64 concurrent requests — and the arithmetic grows 64-fold while the weight traffic does not change at all. Batching is not a throughput trick bolted on the side; it is the only way to raise decode's intensity.

intensity(B) = (2 × P × B) / (2 × P + B × L × 128KB)
                   arithmetic        weights   KV read back

P = 8.03e9 parameters, B = batch size, L = context length in tokens

With a 2,000-token context:

Batch sizeArithmeticBytes movedIntensityVersus A100's ridge of 153

Look at the bytes column. KV traffic grows with the batch while weight traffic does not, so by batch 64 the KV cache is moving more bytes than the weights are. Push the batch to infinity and the weight term vanishes, leaving a hard ceiling:

ceiling = 2 × P / (L × 128KB) = 16.1 GFLOP / 0.262 GB = 61 FLOP/byte

At a 2,000-token context, no batch size on an A100 can reach the compute roof. The ceiling is 61 and the ridge is 153. And you cannot reach 61 either: 80 GB at 90% utilisation leaves 56 GB for KV after the weights, which is 213 requests of 256 MB. That number — not a core count — is your concurrency limit.

Change the context length and the conclusion inverts. At 256 tokens the KV cost per request drops to 33 MB, the ceiling rises to 480 FLOP/byte, and a batch of 512 fits comfortably and runs at 248 FLOP/byte — above the A100 ridge, genuinely compute-bound. Short prompts and long prompts are different engineering problems on the same hardware.

What the arithmetic deliberately ignores

Attention FLOPs, which grow with context and matter at long sequence lengths. Activations and workspace. Non-matmul work, kernel launch overhead and Python. The assumption that each weight and each KV entry is read exactly once per step, which a real cache hierarchy does not honour exactly. This model tells you which regime you are in and roughly how far from the roof. It does not predict your throughput — that is lesson 3's job.

4. Interrogate the metric before you quote it

"We get 2,400 tokens per second" is not yet a fact. Ask which number it is:

MetricWhat it meansWhat it hides
Per-request tokens/secondHow fast one answer streams. The reciprocal of inter-token latency.How many users you can serve. Highest at batch size 1, which is the least economic way to run.
Aggregate output tokens/secondWhat the GPU produces in total. This is the number that divides into cost per million tokens.That it rose because the batch grew, which made every individual user wait longer.
Total tokens/secondInput and output counted together.Everything. A prefill-heavy workload inflates it enormously against a decode-heavy one.

These move in opposite directions. Raising the batch improves aggregate throughput and degrades per-request latency, every time. A single tokens-per-second figure with no concurrency level, no token lengths and no statement of which of the three it is cannot be compared with anything.

Work it yourself

Do this for the model you actually intend to serve — the point is producing your own figure, not re-reading mine. Find its parameter count, layer count, KV head count and head dimension in its config.json, then answer:

  1. Weight bytes at your serving precision, and the decode floor in milliseconds on your GPU.
  2. KV bytes per token, and how many requests at your real context length fit in the memory left over.
  3. Decode intensity at batch 1 and at that maximum batch, against your GPU's ridge point.
  4. Prefill intensity at your p50 input length. Is it above or below the ridge?
A worked check with different inputs

Qwen2.5-7B-shaped: 7.6B parameters, 28 layers, 4 KV heads, head dimension 128, served in bf16 on one A10G.

  • Weights: 7.6e9 × 2 = 15.2 GB. Decode floor: 15.2 ÷ 600 GB/s ≈ 25 ms, so about 39 tokens per second to a single user, before any overhead.
  • KV per token: 2 × 4 × 128 × 2 × 28 = 57,344 bytes ≈ 56 KB — less than half the Llama-3.1-8B figure, because four KV heads instead of eight.
  • 24 GB at 90% leaves 21.6 GB; minus 15.2 GB of weights is 6.4 GB for KV, which is 6.4e9 ÷ (2,000 × 57,344) ≈ 55 requests at 2,000 tokens.
  • Intensity at batch 55: (2 × 7.6e9 × 55) ÷ (15.2 + 55 × 0.057) GB = 836 GFLOP ÷ 18.4 GB ≈ 45 FLOP/byte, against the A10G ridge of 208. Memory-bound by more than 4×, even full.

The lesson from the comparison: the KV head count changed the concurrency limit far more than the parameter count did. Attention shape is a serving decision, not just a modelling one.
